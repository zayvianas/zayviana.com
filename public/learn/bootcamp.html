Module 01

The job, decoded

Plain English

eLend is a mortgage lender that works with home buyers and with mortgage brokers. Like most lenders, they run on a pile of older software that was built over many years. They want someone who can stand between the business people and the tech people, figure out what all the old pieces are and how they connect, draw it clearly, and plan how to replace them without breaking anything the business depends on.

What they actually need

Reading between the lines of the posting, this company needs someone who can:

  1. Untangle the old stuff. Find every legacy app, integration, and process, and map what depends on what.
  2. Write it down well. They say "documentation" in almost every bullet: PRDs, specs, architecture docs, diagrams in Mermaid. Writing is the job.
  3. Plan the move. Roadmaps, migration plans, retirement planning for old systems.
  4. Keep the lights on while you do it. They say business continuity. Mortgages can't stop closing because IT is upgrading.
  5. Run with engineers. Sprint planning, backlog refinement, release planning, deployment readiness.
  6. Use AI to go faster. They want AI-assisted documentation and dependency mapping, which is exactly how you already work.

Their four "top skills" (what Roderick is selling)

1. Fintech

Mortgage is a plus. Your proof: InvestCloud financial apps, Value Tech financing analysis.

2. Technical background

Came from architecture or development. Your proof: Dev10 developer, InvestCloud front-end work, BS in IT, your own apps.

3. Fluent with AI tools

Your proof: Claude Code, MCP, custom GPTs daily, MS in AI.

4. Stakeholder management

Your proof: 10+ person teams, QBRs, MITER workshops across seven departments.

What the interviews will test

They told you exactly what they'll ask you to show: a PRD, a modernization plan, a dependency map, technical writing, architecture docs, a Mermaid diagram, migration and risk planning, and AI-assisted approaches. Modules 04, 06, and 14 cover each one.

Your one-line pitch

"I'm a technical product manager who started as a developer, and most of my work has been modernization: getting old systems, processes, and teams moved to something better without stopping the business. I document everything, and I use AI to do it faster."

Module 02

PM foundations: who does what

Plain English

Picture building a house. The product manager decides what house to build and why: how many bedrooms, who's going to live there, what they need. The project manager makes sure it gets built on time and on budget: schedules the plumber, orders the wood, tracks the money. The technical product manager is a product manager who can also read the blueprints and talk shop with the engineers about the plumbing and wiring.

RoleMain questionOwns
Product ManagerWhat should we build, and why?The problem, the users, priorities, the roadmap, success metrics
Technical PM (TPM)What should we build, and how does it fit the systems we have?All of the above, plus technical requirements, architecture docs, dependencies, tech debt
Project ManagerWill it get done on time and on budget?Schedule, budget, risks, resources, status reporting
Program ManagerHow do several related projects land together?A group of projects with a shared goal, and the dependencies between them
Business AnalystWhat exactly does the business need?Requirements, process maps, gap analysis, UAT support
Product OwnerWhat does the team work on this sprint?The backlog (a Scrum role, often played by the PM)
Scrum MasterIs the team working smoothly?The Scrum process, removing blockers, running ceremonies
Solution ArchitectHow should the pieces be designed?System design, technology choices, integration patterns

The product lifecycle (the big loop)

  1. Discovery. Find the real problem. Talk to users and stakeholders, look at data, map the current state.
  2. Define. Write the problem statement, goals, and success metrics. Decide what's in and out of scope.
  3. Plan. Turn it into a roadmap, then epics and user stories. Find dependencies and risks.
  4. Build. Engineers work in sprints. You answer questions, refine the backlog, and clear blockers.
  5. Test. QA tests it, then the business does UAT (user acceptance testing) to confirm it does what they need.
  6. Launch. Go-live, training, change management, then hypercare (watching it closely right after launch).
  7. Measure and iterate. Check the KPIs. Did it work? What's next?

The SDLC

SDLC means Software Development Life Cycle. It's the same loop from the engineering side: requirements, design, development, testing, deployment, maintenance. When your resume says "full SDLC," it means you've been involved from the first requirement to after launch.

Say it like this

"I've played both the product and the project side. At Tampa Electric I was closer to project and portfolio management: schedules, KPIs, governance. At RevStar I owned the product side: discovery, PRDs, priorities, and what the team built next."

Your proof

RevStar (product + delivery), Tampa Electric (project + portfolio), MITER (program-style integration across many workstreams), InvestCloud (business analyst).

Module 03

Ways of working: Agile, Scrum, Kanban, Waterfall, Hybrid, EOS

Plain English

Waterfall is like baking a wedding cake from a fixed recipe: plan everything, then do each step in order, and you only see the cake at the end. Agile is like cooking for friends and tasting as you go: make a small piece, show it, adjust, repeat. Hybrid is planning the big menu up front (Waterfall) but cooking each dish in small tasted batches (Agile).

Waterfall

Phases in order: requirements, design, build, test, deploy. Big upfront plan, formal sign-offs (stage gates), fixed scope.

Good for: regulated work, hardware, contracts with fixed scope, big enterprise rollouts.

Agile

A mindset: deliver small working pieces often, get feedback, adapt. Values working software and collaboration over heavy documents and rigid plans.

Good for: software where needs change as you learn.

Scrum

The most common Agile framework. Work happens in sprints (usually 2 weeks). Has set roles, events, and artifacts (below).

Kanban

A board with columns (To Do, In Progress, Done). No sprints. Work flows continuously. You limit WIP (work in progress) so things finish instead of piling up.

Good for: support, ops, intake queues.

EOS (Entrepreneurial Operating System)

EOS is a way to run a whole company, not just a dev team. It's popular with small and mid-sized businesses. The pieces you'll mention:

  • Rocks: the 3 to 7 most important priorities for the next 90 days.
  • Level 10 (L10) meeting: a weekly 90-minute leadership meeting with a fixed agenda.
  • Scorecard: 5 to 15 weekly numbers that show if the business is on track.
  • IDS: Identify, Discuss, Solve. How issues get handled in the L10.
  • V/TO: the Vision/Traction Organizer, a two-page company plan.
  • Accountability Chart: like an org chart, but organized by who owns which outcomes.

Hybrid, the way you've done it

Most big companies are hybrid. Leadership plans in quarters and phases (Waterfall-style milestones, or EOS rocks), and the teams deliver in sprints (Agile). Your job is translating between the two: turning quarterly goals into sprint work, and sprint progress into the milestone status leadership wants.

Say it like this

"I'm not religious about methodology. At RevStar I ran Scrum and Kanban inside an EOS company, so the teams worked in two-week sprints while leadership tracked quarterly rocks. At MITER and Tampa Electric the enterprise side had Waterfall-style milestones and approvals, so I ran Agile inside those gates. I pick what fits the work."

Module 04

Requirements: from fuzzy idea to buildable work

Plain English

Requirements are the instructions. If a client says "make loans faster," that's a wish, not something an engineer can build. Your job is asking questions until the wish becomes clear, small, testable pieces like "When a broker uploads a document, the loan file updates within 5 seconds."

The document family

DocWhat it answersWho reads it
BRDBusiness Requirements Document. What the business needs and why.Business leaders
PRDProduct Requirements Document. What we're building, for whom, why, how we'll measure success, what's out of scope.Everyone
Functional specExactly how the feature behaves: screens, rules, edge cases.Engineers, QA
Technical spec / design docHow engineering will build it: architecture, data, APIs.Engineers, architects
SOWStatement of Work. A contract: scope, deliverables, timeline, price.Client, sales, delivery

What goes in a PRD (they'll ask you to write one)

  1. Problem statement. One or two sentences on the pain, and who feels it.
  2. Goals and success metrics. What changes if this works, as numbers (for example, cut loan setup time from 2 days to 2 hours).
  3. Users / personas. Who uses it (borrower, broker, processor, underwriter).
  4. Scope. In scope, and just as important, out of scope.
  5. Requirements. Functional (what it does) and non-functional (speed, security, uptime, compliance).
  6. User stories and acceptance criteria.
  7. Current vs. future state, plus a diagram. This is where Mermaid shows up.
  8. Dependencies, risks, and open questions.
  9. Rollout plan and milestones.

The work breakdown

Initiative (big goal) → Epic (a large chunk of work, weeks to months) → Feature → User story (fits in a sprint) → Task (one person, hours to a day).

User story formula

As a [type of user], I want [to do something], so that [I get some benefit].

Example: "As a mortgage broker, I want to see my loan's status without calling my account rep, so that I can update my borrower right away."

Acceptance criteria (Given / When / Then)

"Given I'm a logged-in broker, when I open a loan, then I see its current stage and the date it entered that stage."

A good story passes INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.

Prioritization tools

  • MoSCoW: Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have (this time).
  • RICE: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. A score for ranking ideas.
  • Value vs. effort: a 2x2. Do high-value, low-effort things first ("quick wins").

Requirements-gathering techniques

Stakeholder interviews, workshops, shadowing people doing their jobs, reviewing existing docs and data, process mapping, and prototypes. "Elicitation" is the fancy word for gathering requirements.

Say it like this

"When I get a vague ask, I start with the problem, not the solution. I'll ask who's affected, what it costs them today, and how we'd know it's fixed. Then I write that into a PRD with clear scope, break it into epics and stories with acceptance criteria, and walk engineering through it before anyone estimates."

Your proof

40+ SOWs and PRDs at RevStar. Acceptance criteria and UAT at Tampa Electric. UAT at InvestCloud.

Module 05

Risk, dependencies, change, and launch

Plain English

A risk is something that might go wrong. An issue is something that already went wrong. A dependency is something you're waiting on, like you can't paint the wall until the drywall's up. A PM keeps a list of all three and has a plan for each.

Tracking it

  • RAID log: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies. One sheet with all four.
  • Risk register: each risk with its likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation (how you'll make it less likely or less bad).
  • Critical path: the chain of tasks that sets the end date. If one slips, the whole project slips.
  • Milestones: big checkpoints (design approved, UAT complete, go-live).
  • RAG status: Red, Amber, Green. The simple traffic light for status reports.
  • Escalation: raising a problem to someone with more authority when you can't solve it at your level. Do it early, with options.

Stakeholder tools

  • RACI: for each decision or task, who is Responsible (does it), Accountable (owns the outcome, only one person), Consulted (gives input), Informed (gets told).
  • Stakeholder map (power/interest grid): high power and high interest people you manage closely, and low/low people you keep informed.

Change management

Software only matters if people use it. Change management is helping people move from the old way to the new way. The common model is ADKAR: Awareness (why change), Desire (want to), Knowledge (how), Ability (can actually do it), Reinforcement (it sticks). In practice: communication plans, training, champions on each team, office hours, and tracking adoption.

Testing and launch words

  • QA: quality assurance, the team or process testing that it works.
  • Unit testing: engineers test small pieces of code.
  • Regression testing: checking that new changes didn't break old features.
  • UAT: real business users test against real scenarios and sign off.
  • Deployment readiness / go/no-go: the checklist and meeting before launch. Testing done? Training done? Rollback plan ready?
  • Cutover: the moment you switch from old to new.
  • Rollback plan: how you go back if launch fails.
  • Hypercare: extra support for the first few weeks after go-live.
Say it like this

"I keep a RAID log from day one and review it weekly. When a risk looks like it's turning into an issue, I escalate early and bring options, not just the problem. Before any go-live I run a go/no-go with a readiness checklist and a rollback plan."

Your proof

Risk logs and training docs at Tampa Electric. Change management and adoption at MITER and Tampa Electric. Budget burn and overrun flags at RevStar.

Module 06

Modernization: the heart of this job

Plain English

A legacy system is like an old house that still works but has knob-and-tube wiring. Everyone's scared to touch it because nobody's sure what's connected to what. Modernization is renovating it while the family still lives there. You map the wiring first (dependency mapping), decide room by room what to fix, replace, or tear out, and you never cut power to the whole house at once.

Core words

  • Legacy system: old software the business still depends on. Often hard to change, poorly documented, and built on old technology.
  • Technical debt: shortcuts and old code that make every future change slower and riskier, like interest on a loan.
  • Digital transformation: the bigger umbrella. Changing how the business works using technology, not just swapping tools.
  • Current state / future state: how it works today vs. how it should work. The difference is the gap analysis, and the gap is your plan.
  • Dependency mapping: finding everything connected to a system. Who uses it, what feeds it data, what it feeds, what reports break if it goes away.
  • Application portfolio: the list of every app the company runs. Application portfolio management means deciding what to keep, invest in, migrate, or kill.
  • Decommission / sunset / retire: turning an old system off for good, after moving its data and users.
  • Business continuity: the business keeps running during the change.

The "7 Rs": what to do with each old app

RMeaningHouse version
RetireTurn it off. Nobody needs it.Tear down the shed
RetainLeave it alone for now.That room's fine
Rehost"Lift and shift." Move it to the cloud as-is.Move the furniture to a new house
RelocateMove it to a different cloud setup with no changes.Move it to the house next door
ReplatformMove it with small tweaks (like a managed cloud database).Move, and swap the old fridge
RepurchaseReplace it with a SaaS product you buy.Buy new furniture instead
Refactor / re-architectRebuild it properly, often as cloud-native or microservices.Full renovation

Another quick model is TIME from Gartner: Tolerate, Invest, Migrate, Eliminate.

Safe ways to migrate

  • Phased rollout: move one group, region, or feature at a time.
  • Parallel run: old and new run side by side so you can compare results before switching.
  • Strangler fig pattern: build new pieces around the old system and route traffic to them bit by bit, until the old one does nothing and you turn it off. (Named after a vine that slowly grows over a tree.)
  • Big bang: switch everything at once. Fast, but risky. Say you'd avoid it for anything customer-facing.
  • Data migration: moving data from old to new. Steps: data mapping (which old field goes to which new field), cleansing, test loads, reconciliation (counts and totals match), final load.

Documenting it

  • Architecture diagram: boxes for systems, arrows for how they talk.
  • C4 model: draw architecture at zoom levels. Context (system + users), Containers (apps, databases), Components, Code. Usually you draw the first two.
  • Data flow diagram: how data moves from system to system.
  • Integration diagram: which systems connect and how (API, file, batch job).
  • Docs-as-code / diagram-as-code: docs and diagrams written as text (Markdown, Mermaid), stored in Git next to the code, updated in the same pull request. See the Mermaid crash course.
  • Governance: the rules for how work gets approved: who signs off, what standards apply, what checkpoints exist.
  • Intake: the front door where new requests come in, get reviewed, and get prioritized.

Your modernization plan template (use this in the interview)

  1. Inventory. List the systems, owners, users, and contracts.
  2. Map dependencies. Draw the current state. Talk to the people who run it daily, because the scariest dependencies are the undocumented ones.
  3. Assess. For each system: business value, technical health, risk, cost. Pick an R.
  4. Design the future state with architecture. Draw it.
  5. Sequence. Lowest-risk, highest-value first. Build a roadmap with milestones.
  6. Migrate safely. Phased or parallel run, data migration with reconciliation, go/no-go, rollback plan.
  7. Retire the old system once usage hits zero and data is archived.
  8. Keep docs alive. Diagrams in the repo, updated with each change.
Say it like this

"Before I touch a legacy system, I want a dependency map I trust. I draw what we know, then I validate it with the people who run it every day, because the integration nobody wrote down is the one that breaks on cutover. Then I sequence the work so we take on low-risk, high-value pieces first and run old and new in parallel where the business can't afford a miss."

Your proof

MITER: aligning legacy systems across two acquisitions. Tampa Electric: modernizing request and incident management in ServiceNow, plus the governance layer. RevStar: intake and governance gates.

Module 07

Tech for PMs: enough to talk shop

Plain English

Think of a restaurant. The front end is the dining room: what customers see and touch. The back end is the kitchen: where the work happens. The database is the pantry: where everything's stored. An API is the waiter: the dining room never walks into the kitchen, it hands the waiter an order in a set format, and the waiter brings back the food.

Architecture basics

  • API: Application Programming Interface. A defined way for one system to ask another for data or an action. REST API is the most common style. It sends requests to URLs called endpoints and usually gets JSON back.
  • Integration: two systems connected so data flows between them. Could be an API, a webhook (system A pings B when something happens), or a batch file sent overnight.
  • Middleware: software that sits between systems and passes messages.
  • Event-driven architecture: systems announce events ("loan funded") and any system that cares reacts. Like a group chat instead of calling each person.
  • Monolith vs. microservices: one big app vs. many small apps that each do one job.
  • Cloud: renting computers from Microsoft (Azure), Amazon (AWS), or Google instead of owning servers. On-prem means servers in your own building.
  • SaaS: Software as a Service. Software you subscribe to online (Salesforce, ServiceNow).
  • Environments: dev (building), test/QA, staging (dress rehearsal), production or "prod" (the real thing).
  • CI/CD: automatically testing and deploying code whenever it changes.
  • ETL: Extract, Transform, Load. Pulling data out of one system, cleaning it, loading it into another.
  • Data warehouse / lakehouse: a central place for reporting data. Microsoft Fabric is one.

Keep it honest

Say "I can read code and build working apps" rather than "I'm an engineer." Your strength is that engineers can't hand-wave you, not that you'd write their production code. That's exactly what a TPM needs.

Module 08

AI vocabulary

Plain English

An AI model like Claude is a very well-read assistant that predicts good next words. It's fast and helpful, but it can sound sure about something wrong. So the rule is: AI drafts, a human checks. Your edge is that you know how to set it up and how to check it.

  • LLM: Large Language Model. The tech behind Claude and ChatGPT.
  • Generative AI: AI that creates text, images, or code.
  • Prompt / prompt engineering: the instructions you give the AI, and the skill of writing them well (context, examples, format).
  • Hallucination: the AI confidently making something up.
  • Human-in-the-loop: a person reviews and approves AI output before it's used.
  • AI agent / agentic workflow: AI that takes multi-step actions with tools (reads files, runs searches, writes drafts), not just answers one question.
  • Claude Code: Anthropic's coding assistant that can read and change code in a project.
  • MCP: Model Context Protocol. A standard way to plug AI into other tools and data (Google Drive, GitHub, databases).
  • Custom GPT: a ChatGPT set up with instructions and files for one job.
  • RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation. The AI looks up your company's documents first, then answers from them.
  • Evals: tests that check whether AI output is good enough.
  • PII: Personally Identifiable Information. In mortgage this is huge: SSNs, income, bank data. Never paste real borrower data into a tool that isn't approved.

How AI helps modernization (their exact ask)

  • Draft PRDs, specs, and user stories from meeting notes.
  • Read old code or config and explain what it does, which speeds up dependency mapping.
  • Generate Mermaid diagrams from notes, which you then verify.
  • Summarize interviews and find patterns.
  • Turn a messy spreadsheet of apps into a clean inventory.
Say it like this

"I use AI to get to a strong first draft fast: PRDs from discovery notes, Mermaid from a whiteboard session, a summary of what an old codebase touches. Then I verify everything with the engineers. AI speeds up the documentation. It doesn't replace checking. And with borrower data, I'd only use tools the company has approved."

Module 09

Mortgage 101

Plain English

A mortgage is a loan to buy a home. The borrower is the buyer. The lender (eLend) lends the money. A broker is a middleman who shops the borrower's loan to lenders. Lending to borrowers through brokers is called wholesale lending. Lending directly to borrowers is retail. eLend's tagline mentions both borrowers and brokers.

The life of a loan

  1. Application. The borrower applies (form 1003, the Uniform Residential Loan Application). Credit is pulled.
  2. Disclosures. The lender sends a Loan Estimate within 3 business days of application.
  3. Processing. A processor collects documents: pay stubs, bank statements, appraisal, title.
  4. Underwriting. An underwriter decides if the loan meets the rules. Often runs through an AUS (automated underwriting system), like Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter or Freddie Mac's Loan Product Advisor. Usually comes back "approved with conditions."
  5. Conditions cleared → Clear to Close (CTC).
  6. Closing. The borrower gets the Closing Disclosure at least 3 business days before closing, then signs.
  7. Funding. Money goes out.
  8. Post-closing and servicing. The loan is often sold on the secondary market, and someone collects the monthly payments (servicing).

Systems you'll hear about

SystemWhat it does
LOSLoan Origination System. The main system where the loan lives from application to closing. Encompass (by ICE Mortgage Technology) is one of the biggest.
POSPoint of Sale. The borrower- or broker-facing portal where applications start.
PPEProduct and Pricing Engine. Figures out which loan products a borrower qualifies for and at what rate.
CRMCustomer Relationship Management. Salesforce often tracks leads, brokers, and relationships.
Doc management / e-signStores loan documents and handles electronic signing.
Data / reportingPipelines and dashboards. Microsoft Fabric and Power BI on Azure fit here.

Compliance words (know they exist, don't overclaim)

  • TRID: the federal disclosure rule behind the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure timing.
  • RESPA and TILA: the two older laws TRID combines.
  • HMDA: lenders report loan data to regulators to check for fair lending.
  • Fair lending: you can't discriminate in who gets loans or at what price.
  • Why it matters to you: changes to the LOS or data flows can break disclosure timing or reporting. Compliance is a stakeholder in every modernization decision.

Things that hurt in mortgage tech (good to ask about)

  • Re-keying the same data into several systems.
  • Custom code and plugins piled onto the LOS over years.
  • Batch jobs and spreadsheets holding reporting together.
  • Rate volume swings: when rates drop, volume spikes and systems get stressed.
Keep it honest

You haven't worked inside a mortgage LOS. Don't pretend you have. Your bridge is fintech plus financing analysis plus fast learning: "I haven't worked in Encompass directly, but I've done UAT on financial applications at InvestCloud and financing analysis for real estate deals, and I've done my homework on how a loan moves from application to funding." Then show you know the lifecycle.

Module 10

Consulting and money words

Plain English

At a consulting firm, you're selling people's time to clients. The SOW is the promise of what you'll deliver. Scope creep is when the client keeps asking for "just one more thing" without paying for it. Burn rate is how fast you're using up the hours or budget.

  • Pre-sales / scoping: conversations before a deal is signed to figure out what the client needs and what it'll cost.
  • SOW: Statement of Work. Scope, deliverables, timeline, assumptions, price.
  • Fixed bid vs. T&M: a set price for set scope, vs. time and materials (billed by the hour).
  • Scope creep: unplanned additions that eat time and margin.
  • Change order / change request: the formal way to add scope, with more time or money.
  • Burn rate / budget burn: hours or dollars used vs. planned.
  • Forecasting / capacity: predicting how many hours you'll need and whether the team has them.
  • Utilization: the share of someone's time that's billable.
  • Margin: what's left after costs. Scope creep kills it.
  • QBR: Quarterly Business Review. A meeting with the client's leaders on results, health, and what's next.
  • KPI: Key Performance Indicator. A number that shows if you're winning.
