    Module 01

    The big picture

    Like you're five

    Joining the Army is like moving to a new country. It has its own pledge (the Creed and the Oath), its own house rules (the Values and General Orders), its own way of saying who's in charge (ranks), and its own language (phonetic alphabet, military time, all the acronyms). Basic training is ten weeks of immersion. You'll learn it all eventually. You'll just have a much easier time if you already speak the language when you land.

    The Blue Book (officially TRADOC Pamphlet 600-4, The Soldier's Blue Book) is the guide every new Soldier gets in Initial Entry Training (IET). It covers the Army Values, ethics, history, organizations, the training you'll go through, pay and leave, and where to get help. It applies to Army National Guard trainees the same as active duty. It's an inspectable item, so expect drill sergeants to ask you about what's in it.

    The name goes back to 1779. Baron von Steuben wrote the Continental Army's first drill manual at Valley Forge, and the printer bound it in the blue paper he had on hand. The name stuck.

    The map

    1. Who you are: the Soldier's Creed, the Warrior Ethos, the Army Values, your oath.
    2. What you do on duty: the three General Orders.
    3. Who's who: enlisted, NCO, warrant, and officer ranks, and how to address each.
    4. How you talk: phonetic alphabet, military time, common terms.
    5. How you carry yourself: attention, parade rest, saluting, the chain of command, battle buddies.
    6. How you stay ready: the Army Fitness Test and the Army Song at every ceremony.
    Module 02

    The words you'll recite

    The Soldier's Creed

    Like you're five

    It's the pledge of allegiance for Soldiers. It starts and ends with the same line, so if you get lost in the middle, you always know how to land.

    1. I am an American Soldier.
    2. I am a warrior and a member of a team.
    3. I serve the people of the United States, and live the Army Values.
    4. I will always place the mission first.
    5. I will never accept defeat.
    6. I will never quit.
    7. I will never leave a fallen comrade.
    8. I am disciplined, physically and mentally tough, trained and proficient in my warrior tasks and drills.
    9. I always maintain my arms, my equipment and myself.
    10. I am an expert and I am a professional.
    11. I stand ready to deploy, engage, and destroy, the enemies of the United States of America in close combat.
    12. I am a guardian of freedom and the American way of life.
    13. I am an American Soldier.
    The real explanation

    The highlighted lines 4 to 7 are the Warrior Ethos. They get pulled out and used on their own all the time. The current Creed was written in 2003 by a Warrior Ethos task force and approved by Army Chief of Staff Gen. Peter Schoomaker. You'll recite it at graduation.

    Memory hook

    Think of it in five chunks. Identity (lines 1 to 3: Soldier, warrior, serve). Warrior Ethos (4 to 7: mission, defeat, quit, comrade, it's a countdown of "never"). Readiness (8 to 9: disciplined, maintain). Profession (10 to 12: expert, ready, guardian). Bookend (13). Five chunks is way easier than thirteen lines.

    Say it like this

    Loud, steady, and together. When a drill sergeant says "Soldier's Creed," the whole platoon starts on "I am an American Soldier." Don't rush ahead of the group.

    The seven Army Values (LDRSHIP)

    Like you're five

    The first letters spell LDRSHIP, which sounds like "leadership." That's the whole trick.

    ValueOfficial short definitionIn your words
    LoyaltyBear true faith and allegiance to the U.S. Constitution, the Army, your unit and other Soldiers.Have your people's backs.
    DutyFulfill your obligations.Do your part, no shortcuts.
    RespectTreat people as they should be treated.Dignity for everyone, including yourself.
    Selfless ServicePut the welfare of the nation, the Army and your subordinates before your own.Others first.
    HonorLive up to Army values.Live all the others, every day.
    IntegrityDo what's right, legally and morally.Same person when nobody's watching.
    Personal CourageFace fear, danger or adversity (physical or moral).Do the right thing when it's hard or unpopular.
    The real explanation

    These are taught in detail during BCT and you'll be expected to give the value and its definition on demand. Know both columns one and two. The third column is just for you. A lot of these sit right on top of what you already believe: selfless service is "the greatest among you will be your servant," and personal courage includes the moral kind, standing for what's right when it's not popular.

    Say it like this

    "Integrity, Drill Sergeant: do what's right, legally and morally."

    The three General Orders

    Like you're five

    When you're put in charge of watching something (the barracks at 0200 on fireguard, a door, a gate), these are your three rules: guard it until someone takes over, follow your instructions, and tell the right person when something's wrong.

    1. I will guard everything within the limits of my post and quit my post only when properly relieved.
    2. I will obey my special orders and perform all my duties in a military manner.
    3. I will report violations of my special orders, emergencies, and anything not covered in my instructions to the commander of the relief.
    The real explanation

    Your post is your area of responsibility. Special orders are the extra instructions just for that post. The commander of the relief is whoever runs the guard shift. The Navy and Marines use 11 General Orders, so if you find a list online with "walk my post in a military manner," that's the wrong branch. The Army uses three.

    Memory hook

    Guard. Obey. Report. First words of each. Then: #1 ends with "properly relieved," #2 ends with "military manner," #3 ends with "commander of the relief."

    Say it like this

    If a drill sergeant asks for your second General Order at 0300 on fireguard: "Drill Sergeant, my second General Order is: I will obey my special orders and perform all my duties in a military manner."

    Your oath (National Guard version)

    Like you're five

    Guard Soldiers have two bosses: the nation and the state. So your oath names both.

    "I, [your name], do solemnly swear (or affirm) that I will support and defend the Constitution of the United States and of the State of Florida against all enemies, foreign and domestic; that I will bear true faith and allegiance to them; and that I will obey the orders of the President of the United States and the Governor of Florida and the orders of the officers appointed over me, according to law and regulations. So help me God."

    The real explanation

    This is the federal law text (32 U.S.C. § 304) with your state filled in. Your actual enlistment paperwork may add a line about the Uniform Code of Military Justice. You already swore it at MEPS. You don't usually have to recite it in basic, but knowing what you promised matters. Notice it ends "So help me God."

    Module 03

    Ranks: who's who and what to call them

    Like you're five

    Enlisted Soldiers wear stripes. Stripes pointing up are chevrons, curved ones underneath are rockers. More stripes, more rank. Officers wear metal: bars, leaves, an eagle, then stars.

    The pay grade (E-1, O-3) is the pay level. The rank is the title (Private, Captain). There are three families: enlisted (E), warrant officers (W), and commissioned officers (O). Corporal and every sergeant are NCOs, noncommissioned officers. They're the ones who'll train you every day.

    Memory hook for officers

    Gold always comes before silver at the same shape. Gold bar, then silver bar (2LT, 1LT). Gold leaf, then silver leaf (MAJ, LTC). Then two bars become "railroad tracks" for Captain, the eagle is Colonel, and generals just count stars from 1 to 4.

    Watch out

    Never call an NCO "Sir" or "Ma'am." Expect the classic "Don't call me sir, I work for a living." In basic, your trainers are always "Drill Sergeant," no matter their actual rank. Unit sizes to know: team (about 4), squad (8 to 16, led by an SSG), platoon (16 to 44, led by a lieutenant and an SFC platoon sergeant), company (60 to 200, captain and first sergeant).

    Numbers

    Say numbers one digit at a time: 1230 is "one two three zero." Say 9 as niner. You'll also hear "tree" for 3 and "fife" for 5 on some radios. Spellings to know: Alfa (not Alpha) and Juliett (two Ts) are the official NATO spellings, though you'll see Alpha everywhere.

    Worked example

    Spelling your last name for in-processing: "Singletary. Sierra, India, November, Golf, Lima, Echo, Tango, Alfa, Romeo, Yankee." Do it right now out loud. Then do your first name. That's your Day 1 drill.

    Military time

    Like you're five

    The day doesn't restart at noon. It keeps counting to 24.

    The rule

    Four digits, no colon, no AM or PM. Midnight is 0000, noon is 1200, 11:59 PM is 2359. For anything 1300 or higher, subtract 12 to get PM time. Say it as "zero six hundred" (0600), "fourteen thirty" (1430), "twenty-one fifteen" (2115). Most Soldiers don't say "hours" after it.

    Try it

    First formation at 0430? That's 4:30 AM. Lights out at 2100? 9:00 PM. Chow at 1730? 5:30 PM. Start setting your phone to 24-hour time today (Settings, General, Date & Time) so it's automatic by November.

    Life rules from the Blue Book

    Your battle buddy goes everywhere with you. Problems go up the chain of command, starting with your team leader, not around it. Start and end everything with the rank: "Yes, Drill Sergeant." Answer questions with "Yes," "No," or "I don't know, but I'll find out." Speak up through SHARP, the chaplain, or the IG if something's wrong. Those exist so you're never stuck.

    Say it like this

    "Drill Sergeant, Private Singletary reports as ordered." (Use whatever your company's standard is. They'll teach you the exact wording on day one.)

    The Army Song

    Like you're five

    Every Army ceremony ends with it, and everyone stands and sings. You already know the tune. It's the "Caissons Go Rolling Along" melody.

    What to know

    Its official title is "The Army Goes Rolling Along." 1st Lt. Edmund Gruber wrote the melody in 1908 in the Philippines as the "Caisson Song" about a horse-drawn artillery battery. John Philip Sousa turned it into a march in 1917. The Army made it the official song in the 1950s with new lyrics. It has an intro verse, a chorus, and a refrain that ends on the title line. The full official version (the one the Army went back to in 2013) has three choruses, but ceremonies usually do the intro, first chorus, and refrain.

    Where to get the words

    I'm not printing the lyrics here. Learn them straight from the Army's own page: army.mil/values/song.html, which has the words and a poster you can download. Save the poster to your phone's photos tonight. The lyrics are short, so play the Army Band recording and sing along twice a day.

    Module 05

    Think like a pro

    How a squared-away trainee thinks

    Drill sergeants aren't testing whether you're smart. They're testing whether you stay calm and accurate under pressure. So practice reciting while tired, while moving, and while someone's interrupting you. Memorize word for word, because "close enough" sounds like not knowing it. And when you don't know something, say so cleanly instead of guessing.

    Common mistakes

    • Learning the Navy or Marine list. Search results mix branches. The Army has three General Orders. Use this page.
    • Calling an NCO "Sir" or "Ma'am." Officers only. NCOs get their rank, and drill sergeants get "Drill Sergeant."
    • Saluting a drill sergeant. You don't salute NCOs. Go to parade rest when they address you.
    • Mixing up SPC and CPL. Same E-4 pay, but the corporal (two chevrons) is an NCO.
    • Cramming the week before. Spread it over three weeks so it's in long-term memory when you're running on four hours of sleep.

    Cheat sheet

    In one sentence

    Know your Creed, your LDRSHIP values, and your three General Orders word for word, read rank at a glance, speak in phonetics and 24-hour time, and stand right when leaders talk to you.

    Remember these

    1. Creed starts and ends with "I am an American Soldier."
    2. Warrior Ethos: mission first, never accept defeat, never quit, never leave a fallen comrade.
    3. LDRSHIP: Loyalty, Duty, Respect, Selfless Service, Honor, Integrity, Personal Courage.
    4. General Orders: Guard, Obey, Report.
    5. Sergeant = 3 chevrons. Rockers: 1 Staff, 2 First Class, 3 Master.
    6. Salute officers. Parade rest for NCOs. "Drill Sergeant" for drill sergeants.
    7. Over 1200? Subtract 12 for PM.

    Words to use

    Battle buddy · Chain of command · Post · Commander of the relief · NCO · Niner · Parade rest · AFT · MOS · AIT

    Watch out for

    • Navy and Marine General Orders online
    • "Sir" to an NCO
    • SPC vs CPL
    • Rushing ahead of the platoon when reciting
    The fitness test, in one line

    The AFT replaced the ACFT on June 1, 2025: 3-rep max deadlift, 2 minutes of hand-release push-ups, sprint-drag-carry, plank, and a two-mile run. Guard Soldiers record it about once a year.
